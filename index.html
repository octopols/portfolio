Three problems that break in non-obvious ways

Each of these is a general class of problem before it is anything I did. I hit a fairly ugly version of all three at BIK.AI, which is where the numbers come from. Short version below; each one has a full writeup.

01 — Queues & throughput

When adding consumers makes a queue slower

More workers should mean more throughput. We scaled the fleet to 38 nodes, the backlog dipped, and then it came back. Consumers were leasing more than they could finish inside the ack deadline, so the broker redelivered — and the autoscaler read those redeliveries as demand and ordered more of the thing causing them.

Nearly 4× the consumers were returning well under 2× the useful work. Rebuilding flow control and backpressure took all 38 nodes off. No capacity was added — it had been there the whole time, being spent twice.

02 — Usage billing

Why usage billing fails quietly instead of loudly

Usage billing has a property ordinary bugs don't: it fails without erroring. Nothing 500s. Credits get granted, invoices generate, and every number is internally consistent with every other number — which is the only kind of wrong that survives in a billing system for long.

Ours was a per-operation rate typed into a free-text field on an internal form. The field was labelled like a price and behaved like a divisor: enter 0.1 where 1 was meant and the account silently gets ten times its allocation. Nobody ever typed "give this customer 10×" — they typed a price. Some accounts were drawing 2–10× what they'd paid for, and no screen anywhere rendered the number that decided it.

Two more in the same system: a 3-hour daily cutoff quietly dropping 12.5% of email billing revenue, and 18:30 UTC hardcoded as midnight across five call sites — correct in India, wrong in every other market it served.

03 — Architecture

How to add a fourth channel without building a fourth pipeline

Multi-channel messaging systems reliably grow one pipeline per channel. Not by decision — because what differs between providers is concrete and sitting in the API docs, while what they share is abstract and only obvious on the third one. So the boundary gets drawn around the channel instead of around the concern.

We had the four-pipeline version across WhatsApp, email, RCS and SMS, and consolidated it onto shared Step Functions orchestration. The proof it worked wasn't the migration — it was RCS, which launched on that infrastructure needing an adapter and nothing else. No scheduler, no retry policy, no status pipeline.

About

Who's writing all this

Most of my job is diagnosis. Something is slow, or wrong, or quietly leaking money, and the fix is rarely the interesting part. The interesting part is working out which of five plausible explanations is the actual one.

The bugs I've enjoyed most are the ones where the obvious answer was backwards. A queue that got worse the more consumers we threw at it. A number that decided every invoice and appeared on exactly zero screens. A timezone constant that was right in one country and wrong in every other one.

Most "scale problems" are correctness problems in a costume.

Idempotency, backpressure and blast-radius isolation have fixed more incidents for me than adding capacity ever has — partly because adding capacity is sometimes how you get the incident.

I learned most of that the unflattering way. The webhook system shipped before I had thought about ack deadlines at all, and merchants found the duplicates for me, which is the worst possible way to find them. The per-store isolation and the health evaluator are both things I built after that call, and both are things I would now build first.

Before this, two Google Summer of Code cycles in C++/Qt — which is how you learn to work in a codebase far older and larger than your understanding of it, mostly by breaking it and then reading a great deal of git blame.

Away from a terminal I shoot film and digital. Mostly street, mostly badly, occasionally something worth keeping.
