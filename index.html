01 — Queues & throughput

When adding consumers makes a queue slower

More workers should mean more throughput. The queue is backed up, you scale the consumer fleet, the backlog dips, and everyone moves on. Then it comes back.

What's usually happening: consumers are leasing more messages than they can finish inside the ack deadline, so the broker redelivers them and the same work gets done twice. That alone is only a discount — it would still drain, slowly. It turns into a wall when every consumer shares one downstream, because each node you add slows the handler on every other node, pushing more messages past their deadline. Useful throughput flattens while the bill keeps climbing. And if you autoscale on queue depth, the autoscaler reads those redeliveries as demand and orders more of the thing causing them.

We hit a fairly complete version of this. Two Pub/Sub subscriptions, 1.26M messages backed up, worst-case delivery running 2.4 hours behind — against a fleet that had grown to 38 nodes and rarely scaled back down. Nearly 4× the consumers were returning well under 2× the useful work. Rebuilding flow control, ack-deadline handling and backpressure dropped the backlog under 4K and worst-case delay to 94 seconds. All 38 nodes came off. No capacity was added — the capacity had been there the whole time, being spent twice.

02 — Usage billing

Why usage billing fails quietly instead of loudly

Metering AI usage is easy — every call can emit an event. Billing it is three harder problems: choosing a unit, reconciling two clocks, and making the arithmetic visible to someone who can notice it's wrong.

Usage billing has a property that ordinary bugs don't: it fails without erroring. Nothing 500s. Credits get granted, usage gets counted, invoices generate, and every number is internally consistent with every other number. The system is confidently wrong, which is the only kind of wrong that survives in a billing system for long. The two structural causes are nearly always the same — a value that decides what someone is charged and appears on no screen, and a unit conversion sitting between what you sold and what you store.

Ours was a good example. The system stored a contracted operation count and a per-operation rate typed into a free-text field on an internal form. The field was labelled like a price and behaved like a divisor: enter 0.1 where 1 was meant, and the account silently gets ten times its allocation. Nobody ever typed "give this customer 10×" — they typed a price. Some accounts were drawing 2–10× what they'd paid for, and no interface anywhere rendered the number that decided it. Rebuilt on a central versioned rate card keyed on tier, country and usage type, with credits denominated in money rather than operation counts. Separately: 12.5% of email billing revenue was being dropped by a 3-hour daily cutoff, and a WhatsApp credit runner had 18:30 UTC hardcoded as midnight across five call sites — correct in India, wrong in every other market it served.

03 — Architecture

How to add a fourth channel without building a fourth pipeline

Multi-channel messaging systems reliably grow one pipeline per channel. Not by decision — because what differs between providers is concrete and sitting in the API docs, while what they share is abstract and only obvious on the third one.

So the boundary gets drawn around the channel instead of around the concern, and scheduling, retry and status tracking get written once per provider. The bill isn't 4× — it's that fixes stop propagating, "delivered" comes to mean four different things, and every cross-channel feature costs four implementations. Drawn correctly, the orchestration owns the lifecycle and the adapter owns only the translation: payload, auth, webhook parsing, declared limits.

We had the four-pipeline version across WhatsApp, email, RCS and SMS, and consolidated it onto shared AWS Step Functions orchestration with Redis-backed segment delivery and a webhook status pipeline. The proof it worked wasn't the migration — it was RCS, which launched on that infrastructure needing an adapter and nothing else. No scheduler, no retry policy, no status pipeline. It carries 2.5M messages a month for 50+ enterprise clients.

About

Who's writing all this

Most of my job is diagnosis. Something is slow, or wrong, or quietly leaking money, and the fix is rarely the interesting part. The interesting part is working out which of five plausible explanations is the actual one.

The bugs I've enjoyed most are the ones where the obvious answer was backwards. A queue that got worse the more consumers we threw at it. A number that decided every invoice and appeared on exactly zero screens. A timezone constant that was right in one country and wrong in every other one.

Most "scale problems" are correctness problems in a costume.

Idempotency, backpressure and blast-radius isolation have fixed more incidents for me than adding capacity ever has — partly because adding capacity is sometimes how you get the incident.

I learned most of that the unflattering way. The webhook system shipped before I had thought about ack deadlines at all, and merchants found the duplicates for me, which is the worst possible way to find them. The per-store isolation and the health evaluator are both things I built after that call, and both are things I would now build first.

Before this, two Google Summer of Code cycles in C++/Qt — which is how you learn to work in a codebase far older and larger than your understanding of it, mostly by breaking it and then reading a great deal of git blame.

Away from a terminal I shoot film and digital. Mostly street, mostly badly, occasionally something worth keeping.
