Three problems worth explaining

All three are production systems at BIK.AI. Two have full writeups; the third is drawn below.

01 — Message delivery

A backlog that 38 nodes were hiding

Two Pub/Sub subscriptions carrying 1.26M messages of backlog, and a fleet that had been grown to absorb it and never scaled back down. The fleet was not undersized — it was feeding the backlog.

Consumers were leasing more messages than they could finish inside the ack deadline, so a fixed share of every node's capacity went to redelivered work. Adding nodes scaled the duplicate work in exact proportion. Rebuilding flow control, ack-deadline handling and backpressure fixed the ratio, and the scale-out came off.

02 — Billing correctness

Money is the only safe unit

I own billing across every revenue channel. The AI billing system I inherited stored a contracted operation count and a per-operation rate typed into a free-text field — so a number that looked like a price behaved like a divisor, and stores were drawing 2–10× their allocation.

Rebuilt onto a central versioned rate card keyed on tier, country and usage type, with credits denominated in money rather than counts. Separately: 12.5% of email billing revenue recovered from a 3-hour daily cutoff, and a WhatsApp credit runner that had 18:30 UTC hardcoded as midnight across five call sites.

03 — Broadcast infrastructure

One delivery path, not four

Every broadcast channel had grown its own scheduling, retry and status-tracking code — four implementations of the same three problems, each with its own bugs.

Consolidating them onto shared AWS Step Functions orchestration made a new channel an adapter rather than a pipeline. That is how RCS shipped: Redis-backed segment delivery and a webhook-driven status pipeline hanging off infrastructure that already existed.

About

Who's writing this

Most of what I do is diagnosis. Something is slow, or wrong, or quietly costing money, and the interesting part is almost never the fix — it's working out which of the five plausible explanations is the real one.

The systems I've enjoyed most were the ones where the obvious answer was wrong: a queue that grew faster the more consumers we added, a billing figure that governed every invoice and appeared on no screen, a timezone constant that was correct in exactly one country.

The through-line, if there is one: most "scale problems" are correctness problems in a costume. Idempotency, backpressure and blast-radius isolation have resolved more incidents for me than adding capacity ever has — partly because adding capacity often is the incident.

Before this, two Google Summer of Code cycles in C++/Qt taught me to work in codebases far older and larger than my understanding of them. Away from a terminal I shoot film and digital, mostly street and mostly badly.
