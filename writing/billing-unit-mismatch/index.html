Billing systems · Notes from production

We sold a count and billed an amount

For months, some customers were quietly getting ten times what they had paid for, and others were being charged a dollar for an operation that cost a fraction of a cent. Rebuilding the system was the easy part. The mismatch underneath it is still there.

[ 01 — What the customer bought ]

Ten thousand operations

I work on the platform side of a company that runs marketing campaigns for e-commerce brands. Part of what we sell is AI: agents that answer customer messages, write copy, handle voice calls. Customers buy it in the simplest unit anyone could ask for.

10,000 AI operations per month.

No credits. No currency. No mention of which model runs, or what it costs us when it does. A number of things the AI will do for you, per month. That is the whole promise, and it is a good one — it is the only version of this a non-technical buyer can hold in their head.

Everything that went wrong afterwards comes from the gap between that sentence and what a billing ledger can actually store.

[ 02 — What the system stored ]

A pool and a rate

The first version stored two numbers per customer: a creditPool, which was usually just the contracted operation count, and a baseRate per operation, typed in by whoever set the account up. Operations available was the division of one by the other. 

creditPool = 10,000        (the contracted count, stored directly)

rate = 1.0  →  10,000 / 1.0 =  10,000 ops   correct
rate = 0.5  →  10,000 / 0.5 =  20,000 ops   2× the contract
rate = 0.1  →  10,000 / 0.1 = 100,000 ops   10× the contract

The intended value was always 1. The field was free text on an internal form, filled in by account managers who had no reason to know that this particular number was load-bearing. It was labelled like a price. It behaved like a divisor.

So a rate of 0.1 did not make anything cheaper. It multiplied the customer's allocation by ten.

[ 03 — Why nobody noticed ]

The screen showed the contract, not the consequence

Here is the part I keep coming back to. The bug was visible in the data from the first day. It was never visible on a screen.

Every interface — the account manager's view, the customer's dashboard — displayed the contracted number. 10,000. The figure that actually governed consumption, pool / rate, was never rendered anywhere. It existed only as an intermediate value inside a deduction that ran nightly and printed nothing.

Nobody was ignoring a warning sign. There was no warning sign. A customer on a 0.1 rate used a hundred thousand operations against a ten thousand operation contract, and every screen either party could look at agreed that everything was fine.

A derived value that governs money and is displayed nowhere is not a calculation. It is an unexploded assumption.

[ 04 — The other direction ]

A dollar an operation

The same free-text field failed the opposite way for customers billed in dollars. Some accounts were set up with rate = 1 — read as "one unit per operation", which sounds like the sensible default. In a pool denominated in dollars, it means one dollar per operation.

This stayed invisible for as long as the free allocation lasted, because deductions came out of the free pool and nobody was being invoiced. The moment a customer exhausted their free credits, every subsequent operation billed a dollar from their main wallet — several orders of magnitude above what the underlying model call cost.

It went undetected for the most boring reason available: in that version, almost nobody ever finished their free credits. The bug needed a heavy customer to surface, and heavy customers were exactly who we did not have yet.

[ 05 — The rebuild ]

Credits become money

The second version changed four things. Only one of them is interesting.

The boring three: rates moved into a single central, versioned rate card, keyed by tier × country × usage type, editable only through an admin console with an audit log — account managers can no longer type a rate at all. Margin became an explicit per-customer markup percentage, stored separately, so the base rate always reflects real cost. And voice became a first-class dimension, because a voice vendor charges per second and no amount of pretending makes a second an operation.

The interesting one: the free credit pool stopped being a count and became an amount of money. 

allocatedFreeCredits = contractedOps × finalRate     // in the customer's currency

The nightly job now deducts currency, not counts. Every unit of usage is priced through the rate card, marked up, and subtracted from a balance denominated in dollars or rupees. This is correct in a way the first version could not be: a rate change no longer silently re-scales somebody's entitlement, because entitlement is money and money does not care what the rate is.

It also created the problem that the rest of this piece is about.

[ 06 — Migration ]

The dashboards were the audit

Alongside the rebuild we shipped a usage dashboard: spend per customer, usage per feature, billed against free, trends over time. It was meant to be an operational convenience.

What it actually did was make the first version's arithmetic visible for the first time. Migrating old contracts meant translating a count and a rate into an amount, and the old bugs came through the translation intact and compounded — a customer on a 0.1 rate migrated to a credit pool ten times larger than it should have been. Every migrated account had to be recalculated against the new rate card and normalised, by hand.

That manual audit was the direct cost of never having had a central place where rates lived. Not the bug itself — the bug was one bad divisor. The cost was that there was no single thing to look at to find out how many accounts it had touched.

[ 07 — The first thing we can't fix ]

An exchange rate that will not hold still

The rate card is in dollars. Customers billed in rupees have a wallet in rupees. On billing day the dollar rate is converted at that day's exchange rate, and the deduction is made in rupees. That part is right — the customer pays rupees at the rate that applied on the day the usage happened.

The trouble is the other direction. The contract promises a count, so somewhere a screen has to turn a rupee balance back into operations: 

balance = ₹42,500        (50,000 ops at $0.01, converted at 85)

at 85:  floor(42,500 / 0.85) = 50,000 ops      as promised
at 88:  floor(42,500 / 0.88) = 48,295 ops      1,705 short
at 90:  floor(42,500 / 0.90) = 47,222 ops      2,778 short
at 95:  floor(42,500 / 0.95) = 44,736 ops      5,264 short

Nothing has been deducted between those four lines. The wallet holds the same ₹42,500 in all of them. The only thing that moved was the currency market, and a customer who was sold fifty thousand operations watches the number on their dashboard drift down through the month.

The billing is financially correct throughout. The display is not reliably aligned with the contract, and it cannot be, because the contract is denominated in a unit the ledger does not store and the bridge between them is a number neither side controls.

Unresolved

The contract is in operations. The wallet is in rupees. The exchange rate between them is not fixed, so there is no honest way to render one as the other. Voice sidesteps this by being displayed as credits — but only because "one voice operation" was never a meaningful unit to begin with.

[ 08 — The second thing we can't fix ]

Five pricing tiers, all set to the same number

The rate card supports five model tiers, so that a cheap model and an expensive one can be priced differently. It works. It is tested. Every tier is currently set to the same rate, and we cannot change that.

Consider two customers, both sold ten thousand operations. One spends theirs on a cheap tier, one on an expensive tier. If tiers carry different rates, the same monetary allocation yields a different number of operations for each — and both were promised the same count. Worse, a single customer's total shifts month to month depending on which features they happened to use.

So the tier infrastructure sits there, fully built, deliberately flattened. It is not a technical limitation. It is what happens when the pricing model can express something the sales promise cannot.

You cannot price by model tier while selling by operation count. Pick one. We shipped both and had to disable the half that was working.

[ 09 — The quiet ones ]

Two gaps that only show up if you look

Renewal doesn't refill anything. When a billing cycle rolls over, the contract's start and end dates update automatically. The free credit balance does not. Nothing resets it and nothing re-provisions the new cycle's allocation — somebody has to call an admin endpoint per customer, every cycle. Miss it, and the customer enters the new month either with last month's leftovers or with nothing, billing straight to their main wallet with no free buffer in front of it.

A missing flag means free usage. Every usage event carries a boolean marking whether it belongs to a free agent or a billable one. The nightly job requires it to be explicitly true or false. If a feature emits an event without it, those events fall into the aggregation's missing bucket and are skipped — no error, no alert, no charge.

The dashboard has a column for it. We call it loss: real usage, correctly recorded, never billed. It is the only place that number appears, and it only appears if someone opens the page and reads it.

[ 10 — What I'd carry forward ]

Three things I believe more strongly now

Render every number that governs money. The original bug was one bad divisor, and it survived for months purely because the value it produced was never drawn on a screen. The fix that mattered most was not the rebuild — it was the dashboard that made the arithmetic visible. If a derived figure decides what someone is charged, it belongs in the interface, even an ugly internal one.

A free-text numeric field on an internal form is a production input. It deserves the validation, the audit trail and the review you would give an API. The rate field had none, because it looked like configuration rather than code.

Decide what the unit is before you build the ledger. Everything still open here — the exchange-rate drift, the flattened tiers — traces back to selling a count and storing an amount. Both models are defensible. Holding both at once means permanently maintaining a conversion that cannot be made honest, and no amount of engineering downstream repairs a unit mismatch decided in a sales conversation.
